## 🏔️ Beyond Rough Heston: Quadratic and Hybrid Rough Volatility

The previous answer mentioned rough Heston as a phase‑two upgrade. The frontier has moved further.

### Quadratic Rough Heston (QRH) and QRH+
A 2026 study on **0DTE SPX options** compared four rough‑volatility specifications—Rough SABR, Rough Bergomi, **Quadratic Rough Heston (QRH)**, and **QRH+**—against a non‑rough jump‑diffusion benchmark under a bid–ask constrained calibration framework. The key findings are directly relevant to weekly Nifty options:

- **QRH+ fits best when the smile shows pronounced downside curvature**—exactly the regime Indian markets enter during sharp sell‑offs or event‑driven panics.
- However, a **well‑specified non‑rough benchmark matches or outperforms rough models on average** when calibration is done on equal terms. This is an important epistemic caution: rough volatility is not a universal panacea; it is a **regime‑specific tool**.
- The study explicitly notes that classical Heston, Bergomi, and SABR produce **bounded or insufficiently steep ATM skews as maturity tends to zero**, making them structurally ill‑suited to the 0DTE/ultra‑short regime.

**Integration path for Nifty weeklies:** Use QRH+ as a **regime‑conditional advisor**, not a replacement for Black‑76. Fit it only when the observed smile exhibits strong downside curvature (e.g., when the 25‑delta put‑call skew exceeds a rolling percentile threshold). When curvature is moderate, the non‑rough benchmark may be more reliable.

### The 4/2 Rough and Smooth Model
A 2025 paper proposes a **4/2 rough and smooth stochastic volatility model** that combines **rough Heston (roughness 1/2)** with a **smooth 3/2 model** through a convex specification. This parsimonious two‑factor model admits semi‑closed‑form pricing and captures both the rough short‑end dynamics and the smoother long‑end behavior. For weekly options, the **3/2 component** is particularly useful because it generates **explosive volatility behavior** during stress—something Heston cannot do.

**Why it matters:** Weekly Nifty options spend most of their life in a moderate‑vol regime but occasionally experience violent vol spikes around events (RBI policy, budget, global risk‑off). A model that can smoothly transition between rough and smooth/explosive regimes is better suited than a single‑factor rough Heston.

### Fast Rough Heston Calibration
A 2025 paper by Boyarchenko and Levendorskii combines a **modified Adams method** with **SINH‑acceleration** of Fourier inversion to price vanilla options under rough Heston in **tens to hundreds of milliseconds** even for near‑expiry and far‑OTM options. They also warn about **“ghost calibration”** —where numerical error and model specification error offset each other, creating an apparently good fit that vanishes with a more accurate pricer.

**Integration path:** Use this fast calibration method to compute **model‑consistent Greeks** under rough Heston for the nearest weekly expiry. Log both Black‑76 and rough‑Heston deltas/gammas in the edge snapshot; let the learning loop decide which Greeks are more predictive. The ghost‑calibration warning means you should validate the calibrated surface against **out‑of‑sample strikes** before trusting it.

## 🧬 Signature Methods and Rough Path Theory

This is arguably the most mathematically advanced direction available—and it directly addresses the **path‑dependent nature of weekly options**, where the entire intraday price path matters, not just the current spot and vol.

### Signature‑Based Optimal Stopping for American‑Style Decisions
Your system makes **entry/exit decisions** that are structurally similar to **optimal stopping problems**. A 2025 paper in *Finance and Stochastics* proposes two **signature‑based methods** to solve optimal stopping in **non‑Markovian frameworks**—exactly the setting of rough volatility.

- The **primal formulation** generalises the famous **Longstaff–Schwartz algorithm** using **linear functionals of rough‑path signatures** as the regression basis.
- The **dual formulation** parametrises square‑integrable martingales using signature functionals and applies sample average approximation.

**Why this is revolutionary for your system:** Your current entry logic is a Markovian rule—it looks at the current snapshot of features. But weekly option payoffs depend on the **entire intraday path**: the sequence of impulses, the evolution of order‑book imbalance, the timing of wall breaks. Signature methods provide a **principled, finite‑dimensional representation of the path** that can be used as features in your learning loop or directly in an optimal‑stopping policy.

### Deep Signature Approach for Non‑Markovian Volatility
A 2025 paper reformulates asset dynamics as a **rough stochastic differential equation** and represents the rough paths via **time‑extended Brownian motion signatures**, transforming the rough SDE into a classical SDE. They propose a **deep signature approach** for both linear and nonlinear representations and prove convergence.

**Practical implication:** This gives you a **computationally tractable way to price and hedge under rough volatility** without Monte Carlo simulation of fractional Brownian motion. You can use signature‑based features (e.g., iterated integrals of the futures price path up to depth 3 or 4) directly in a gradient‑boosted model or a neural network, capturing path‑dependent effects that no point‑in‑time feature can.

### Signature‑Based American Option Pricing Under Rough Volatility
A 2025/2026 line of work extends signature‑based primal and dual optimal stopping to **American options under rough volatility**, using **deep‑signatures and signature‑kernels**. If you ever move to American‑style Nifty options (or want to model the **early‑exercise premium** embedded in weekly options), this is the state‑of‑the‑art framework.

## 🧠 Neural Networks with Hard Financial Constraints

The previous answer suggested gradient‑boosted trees and RL. The frontier goes further: **architectures that are structurally incapable of producing arbitrage**.

### Finance‑Informed Neural Networks (FINN)
A 2025/2026 paper proposes a **Finance‑Informed Neural Network (FINN)** that integrates financial theory directly into the learning objective. The key theoretical result: **minimising replication error recovers the arbitrage‑free pricing operator** and yields **economically meaningful sensitivities**.

**What this means for you:** Instead of training a model to predict option prices and then hoping it doesn't violate no‑arbitrage, FINN trains a model whose **loss function itself enforces no‑arbitrage** through replication. The sensitivities (Greeks) that emerge are **model‑consistent by construction**—no finite‑difference bumping required.

### DeepOption with Transformer and Arbitrage‑Free Constraints
A 2026 IEEE paper introduces a hybrid framework extending the **DeepOption architecture** with three innovations:
1. A **Transformer with time‑decay positional encoding** to adaptively focus on recent market data while capturing long‑term dependencies.
2. A **multi‑constraint loss function** enforcing no‑arbitrage, strike monotonicity, and other economic principles.
3. A **two‑stage transfer learning** strategy: pre‑train on synthetic data from classical models, then fine‑tune on scarce real‑market data.

**Why the Transformer matters:** Your edge snapshot is a **time series of features**. A Transformer with time‑decay attention can learn that recent order‑flow imbalances are more predictive than older ones, and can capture **long‑range dependencies** across the trading session—something hand‑crafted z‑scores cannot.

### Arbitrage‑Free Neural SDE Market Models
A 2023 paper (still state‑of‑the‑art) calibrates **Neural SDE models** that are **guaranteed to satisfy a set of linear inequalities** corresponding to no‑arbitrage. The key technique is a **hard constraint approach** that modifies the network to respect sufficient conditions on the drift and diffusion to restrain the process within a **convex polytope** of arbitrage‑free states.

**Practical value:** A Neural SDE market model gives you a **generative simulator of the entire option surface** that is arbitrage‑free by construction. You can use it for:
- **Stress‑testing** your entry logic on synthetic surfaces that preserve no‑arbitrage.
- **Data augmentation** for the learning loop—generating realistic but unseen market scenarios.
- **Value‑at‑Risk** estimation for your paper book.

### Neural Spline Flows and Signature‑Augmented Market Simulators
A PhD thesis (2026) develops a **neural spline flow**—a conditional density model built from neural‑network‑based spline constructions—to simulate **arbitrage‑free option markets** for Deep Hedging. They **extend the flow with path signatures from rough path theory**, proving that the model becomes **convex in its parameters during calibration**, mitigating local minima.

This is a powerful combination: **generative modelling + rough path features + convex calibration**. It produces a **real‑world measure simulator** (not just risk‑neutral) that can be used to train RL agents without overfitting to historical data.

## 🎯 Deep Hedging and Reinforcement Learning for Risk Management

The previous answer mentioned RL for entry timing. The frontier is **Deep Hedging**—using RL to learn the **hedging policy itself**, not just the entry signal.

### RL‑Based Deep Hedging Under Market Frictions
A 2025 paper presents a practical RL framework for **dynamic hedging of equity index option exposures** under **realistic transaction costs and position limits**. Another 2025 study finds that **TD3 and SAC agents** outperform traditional delta hedging, achieving **2/3 reductions in expected hedging losses** and **over 50% reductions in 5th‑percentile CVaR**.

**Why this matters for a buy‑only weekly strategy:** Even a “buy‑only” strategy has implicit hedging decisions—when to exit, whether to roll, how to size. Deep Hedging learns a **state‑contingent policy** that minimises a **convex risk measure** (e.g., CVaR) rather than expected P&L. This directly aligns with your goal of avoiding “stupid non‑riding trades”: the agent learns to **cut losers early** because the CVaR penalty for holding through a drawdown is high.

### Deep Hedging with Implied Volatility Surface Feedback
A 2025 paper enhances Deep Hedging by **integrating implied volatility surface dynamics** into the rebalancing decisions via a **deep policy gradient algorithm**. The agent observes not just the spot and current Greeks, but the **evolution of the surface**—skew changes, term‑structure shifts, curvature movements.

**Integration path:** Train a Deep Hedging agent on your existing paper‑book data, using the **edge snapshot** (which already contains IV, GIM, wall levels, flow imbalances) as the state. Use **CVaR** as the risk criterion rather than raw P&L. Register the learned policy as an **advisor** for exit timing and position sizing. The SPRT framework can validate whether the Deep Hedging policy genuinely improves the champion book.

### Imitation Learning from Black‑Scholes
A 2025 paper introduces **Imitation Learning Deep Hedging (ILDH)**, which bridges Black‑Scholes‑Merton with deep RL by **imitating the BSM delta** as a warm‑start policy. This is a practical trick: rather than training an RL agent from scratch (which is sample‑inefficient), initialise it to mimic a known‑reasonable baseline, then let it improve under market frictions.

## 📊 Hawkes Processes: The Microstructural Foundation of Rough Volatility

The previous answer mentioned VPIN. The frontier is **Hawkes processes**—self‑exciting point processes that model the **clustering of order arrivals**.

### Hawkes‑Based Rough Volatility
A line of research shows that modelling **market order flow with linear Hawkes processes** and imposing **no‑arbitrage plus non‑trivial market impact** implies that the **volatility process must be rough**. This is a profound result: **rough volatility is not an assumption—it emerges endogenously from microstructure**.

### Quadratic Hawkes Processes
A class of **microscopic models where order flows are driven by quadratic Hawkes processes** generates **rough volatility, leverage effects, and rough Heston‑type limits**. The Hawkes kernel captures the **self‑excitation and cross‑excitation** between buy and sell orders.

### Multivariate Hawkes for Limit Order Book Option Pricing
A 2024 paper prices European and spread options using a **multivariate Hawkes‑based model of the limit order book**, revealing the **relationship between stock volatility and implied order flow**. The model reproduces the **skew, smile, and term structure** of the implied volatility surface from order‑flow primitives.

**Integration path for your system:** Fit a **Hawkes model to the options order flow** (buy/sell market orders in the option contracts themselves) and use the **branching ratio** (the proportion of endogenous vs. exogenous activity) as a **toxicity / informed‑trading feature**. A high branching ratio means current flow is largely self‑generated (herding), which is likely **noise**, not information. A low branching ratio with high intensity means **exogenous information** is arriving. This directly distinguishes “stupid non‑riding trades” from genuine informed flow.

## 🌀 Stochastic Local Volatility (SLV) with Monte Carlo Calibration

The previous answer mentioned SVI. A more powerful alternative is **Stochastic Local Volatility (SLV)** , which combines a **local volatility component** (to exactly reprice vanillas) with a **stochastic volatility component** (to produce realistic forward smiles and dynamics).

- **Local volatility** alone reprices vanillas exactly but produces **unrealistic implied volatility dynamics**.
- **Stochastic volatility** alone produces realistic dynamics but has **trouble repricing all vanilla prices**.
- **SLV** combines both: the local component handles the static smile, the stochastic component handles the dynamics.

A 2020 paper provides a **new calibration of the Heston SLV model** with a **parallel GPU implementation**. Another line of work develops **Monte Carlo calibration algorithms** for SLV models with deterministic and stochastic interest rates.

**Why SLV matters for weekly options:** The **leverage function** in SLV directly captures the relationship between spot moves and volatility moves. For weekly Nifty options, this relationship is **highly nonlinear** (the “leverage effect” is strong for index options). A calibrated SLV model gives you **model‑consistent vanna and charm**—the second‑order Greeks that matter most near expiry.

## 🧮 Path‑Dependent Volatility Models

A 2025/2026 paper details calibration procedures for **path‑dependent volatility models** where the **local volatility component depends on the running maximum** of the underlying. This is a **non‑Markovian** specification that captures the empirical observation that volatility behaves differently after large drawdowns.

For a buy‑only weekly options strategy, the **running maximum** is a natural state variable: after a sharp rally, the probability of a continued rally may be lower (mean reversion), and the volatility surface may be flatter. A path‑dependent volatility model can capture this **asymmetry** that a Markovian model cannot.

## ⚛️ Quantum Computing for Option Pricing (Horizon Technology)

Quantum **Amplitude Estimation (QAE)** achieves **quadratic convergence** over classical Monte Carlo: classical MC error decays as \(O(1/\sqrt{N})\), while QAE error decays as \(O(1/N)\). This means a **100× speedup** in the number of paths required for the same accuracy.

A 2026 paper provides an **end‑to‑end pipeline for multidimensional option pricing** using **Quantum‑Accelerated Monte Carlo (QAMC)** based on QAE, achieving quadratic convergence improvements over classical MC.

**Practical status:** Fault‑tolerant quantum hardware capable of running QAE for realistic option pricing is likely **years away**. However, **quantum‑inspired classical algorithms** (e.g., tensor‑network‑based amplitude estimation) are already available and can provide some of the same benefits on classical hardware. This is a **watch‑and‑experiment** direction, not a near‑term production upgrade.

## 💎 The Deepest Structural Insight

The most advanced models converge on a single theme: **the path is the primitive, not the point**. Rough volatility, signature methods, Hawkes processes, and path‑dependent volatility all agree that **the sequence of events matters**—not just the current spot and vol. Your system already records tick‑level data and has a learning loop; the limiting factor is not data, but the **mathematical representation of the path**. Signature methods offer the most principled finite‑dimensional representation; Hawkes processes offer the most microstructurally grounded; Deep Hedging offers the most decision‑theoretically coherent. Combining a **Hawkes‑derived toxicity feature** with **signature‑based path features** and a **CVaR‑optimised Deep Hedging exit policy** would put your system at the genuine research frontier—not just in retail, but in quantitative finance generally.
